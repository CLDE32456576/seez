## CATEGORY 1 — OPENING / FIRST IMPRESSION (Concepts 1–14)

### 1. The Permission-Based Opener (PBO)
**Source:** 30 Minutes to President's Club (Cegelski & Farrokh) / Gong data
**Core idea:** Acknowledge the interruption out loud and *ask permission* to continue. The first 60 seconds of a cold call exists to "earn the next 60 seconds." Gong's data shows PBOs convert ~10x better than "How are you?"
**Why it works psychologically:** Reactance theory — when freedom is threatened (being pitched), people resist. By naming the interruption and giving the prospect control, you remove the threat, and the buyer's System 1 stops scanning for "telemarketer."
**How to apply:** Open with transparent acknowledgement + a compressed permission ask + a clear value beat.
**Script:** *"Hey [Name], totally a cold call — can I have 27 seconds to tell you why I called and you can hang up if it's irrelevant?"*
**Stage:** Cold Call
**Common mistakes:** Asking "How's your day?" (triggers defenses); apologizing weakly ("Sorry to bother you…"); rushing past the permission and pitching anyway.

### 2. The Pattern Interrupt
**Source:** Sales conversation intelligence (Gong/Chris Orlob); behavioral psychology
**Core idea:** Most prospects answer cold calls in "auto-reject" mode. A pattern interrupt — an unexpected phrase, tone, or admission — forces System 2 to engage so the brain stops running its rejection script.
**Why it works:** Novelty captures attentional resources (Cialdini's "the different" attention magnet). When the brain can't predict the next move, it has to listen.
**How to apply:** Use disarming honesty: "This is a cold call" or an unusual question that breaks the salesperson script.
**Script:** *"I'll be honest, this is a cold call — you can hang up, or you can give me 30 seconds. Most people give me the 30."*
**Stage:** Cold Call
**Common mistakes:** Using "interrupts" that feel gimmicky; over-using the same line so it becomes its own pattern.

### 3. The "Heard Our Name Tossed Around" Opener
**Source:** 30MPC (Cegelski & Farrokh)
**Core idea:** Establish peer-level credibility immediately by referencing two competitors the prospect respects.
**Why it works:** Social proof + authority transfer (Cialdini). If two of their peers use you, you must be considered. It also frames the call as a category conversation, not a vendor pitch.
**How to apply:** Pre-research two recognizable customers in their segment.
**Script:** *"Hey [Name], we work with [Competitor A] and [Competitor B] — have you heard our name tossed around?"*
**Stage:** Cold Call / Discovery
**Common mistakes:** Name-dropping companies the prospect doesn't recognize; bragging instead of inviting a response.

### 4. Dangerous Specificity
**Source:** 30MPC
**Core idea:** Replace vague benefit language ("we help with efficiency") with hyper-specific, peer-relevant outcomes ("we help law firms cut 14 hours/week of back-office entry").
**Why it works:** Specificity triggers cognitive ease and credibility (Kahneman); vague claims trigger BS-detection. Specific numbers presuppose evidence.
**How to apply:** Build a "problem proposition" library by ICP — vivid, emotionally charged problem + measurable outcome.
**Script:** *"I help VPs of Sales whose reps are spending 9+ hours a week on CRM admin instead of selling — that ring a bell?"*
**Stage:** Cold Call / Email
**Common mistakes:** Defaulting to "optimize/streamline/AI-powered" buzzwords; quoting impossibly perfect numbers (10x return) that read as fake.

### 5. The Problem Proposition (vs. Value Proposition)
**Source:** Nick Cegelski (30MPC)
**Core idea:** Lead with a vivid, emotionally charged problem your buyer feels — *then* briefly hint at the solution. The buyer's brain is problem-aware long before it's solution-aware.
**Why it works:** Loss aversion (Kahneman/Tversky) — pain frames make brains pay 2x the attention of gain frames. Problems also pass the "is this me?" filter faster than features.
**How to apply:** Convert every value prop into a problem statement first.
**Script:** *"Most VPs of CS we talk to are watching renewals slip because they only see churn risk in the last 30 days — and by then it's too late."*
**Stage:** Cold Call / Cold Email / Demo opening
**Common mistakes:** Listing features; describing the problem in your jargon, not theirs.

### 6. The PPO Agenda (Purpose, Plan, Outcome)
**Source:** 30MPC Discovery framework
**Core idea:** Open every meeting (after pleasantries) by stating the **Purpose** of the call, the **Plan** for how time will be spent, and the **Outcome** you're aiming for, then ask if anything should be added.
**Why it works:** Reduces ambiguity (humans hate ambiguity more than rejection — Sandler). Establishes you as the calm, prepared expert. Triggers commitment/consistency: once they agree to the plan, they'll cooperate with it.
**How to apply:** Standardize a 30-second open for every discovery and demo.
**Script:** *"Quick agenda — purpose today is to see if a closer look at [X] makes sense. Plan: I'll ask 10–15 minutes of questions, then walk you through how peers solve this. Outcome: by the end you'll either want a next step or you'll tell me 'no thanks.' Anything you'd add?"*
**Stage:** Discovery / Demo
**Common mistakes:** Burying agenda mid-call; making it a monologue; never inviting their additions.

### 7. The First-Five-Minutes Credibility Block
**Source:** 30MPC Discovery Course
**Core idea:** The first 5 minutes are not for warm-up — they're for establishing that you've earned the next 25. Demonstrate market understanding, not personal charm.
**Why it works:** Cialdini's authority principle — credibility built early acts as a halo effect over the rest of the call. Buyers decide within minutes whether to engage System 1 (autopilot dismissal) or System 2 (real evaluation).
**How to apply:** Open with: a specific peer trend, a relevant insight, and a hypothesis about their world.
**Script:** *"Before I ask anything, here's what I'm seeing across 40+ revenue leaders this quarter: pipeline coverage is up but win rates are down 6%. I assumed that might be live for you too — fair?"*
**Stage:** Discovery
**Common mistakes:** Confusing rapport ("how's the weather?") with credibility; opening with company history.

### 8. The Pre-Suasive Question (Privileged Moment)
**Source:** Cialdini, *Pre-Suasion*
**Core idea:** Ask a single question *before* the pitch that channels the prospect's attention to a state aligned with saying yes (e.g., "Do you consider yourself someone who values being ahead of the curve?"). This redirects focus and creates a "privileged moment."
**Why it works:** "What's focal is presumed causal." Once attention is channeled toward a self-image, behavior conforms to it (commitment/consistency).
**How to apply:** Front-load one identity-priming question before any ask.
**Script:** *"Quick one before we dive in — would you describe yourself as someone who's actively trying to fix [X], or more in 'good-enough' mode right now?"*
**Stage:** Cold Call / Discovery opening
**Common mistakes:** Manipulative phrasing that feels like a quiz; asking after the pitch (too late).

### 9. The "Sell the Test Drive, Not the Car"
**Source:** 30MPC
**Core idea:** Lower commitment friction on the cold call by framing the next step as exploration, not purchase.
**Why it works:** Foot-in-the-door / commitment escalation. Tiny initial yeses prime larger yeses. It also disarms reactance — "I'm not asking you to buy."
**Script:** *"My guess is this isn't your top priority, and that's totally fine. Would you be open to a 20-minute look — even just so you have us on a shortlist if budget shifts?"*
**Stage:** Cold Call / Email CTA
**Common mistakes:** Selling the car (asking for budget/decision on first touch).

### 10. The Triple Touch Opener (Calendar-Day Stack)
**Source:** 30MPC / Fanatical Prospecting (Blount)
**Core idea:** Stack a phone call, email, and LinkedIn message on the same day for the same prospect to dramatically improve response rates.
**Why it works:** Mere-exposure effect (familiarity bias) + availability heuristic — three touches in one day make you "the rep who keeps showing up" rather than spam.
**How to apply:** Build sequences in your dialer/email tool to fire on the same day.
**Script:** Voicemail: *"Left you a quick email and LinkedIn — wanted to make sure it landed."*
**Stage:** Cold Outreach
**Common mistakes:** Spreading touches across 7 days (forgettable); identical messaging across channels.

### 11. The Trigger-Event Opener
**Source:** Fanatical Prospecting + 30MPC ("30 seconds to disqualify, 30 to research")
**Core idea:** Open every cold outreach with a relevant trigger (funding, hire, earnings call, LinkedIn post, layoff). The trigger answers "why now" and "why me."
**Why it works:** Relevance primes attention (Pre-Suasion). Triggers also signal you did 30 seconds of homework, which raises perceived authority.
**Script:** *"Saw you just hired your first VP of Customer Success — usually that means [X problem]. Reason for the call…"*
**Stage:** Cold Call / Cold Email
**Common mistakes:** Mentioning trigger but not connecting it to a problem; using stale triggers (>30 days old).

### 12. The Permission Opener for Discovery (Sandler Up-Front Contract)
**Source:** Sandler Selling System
**Core idea:** Verbally agree, before discovery, on (1) Purpose, (2) Their Agenda, (3) Your Agenda, (4) Time, (5) Outcome — *including the right to say "no."*
**Why it works:** Removes ambiguity (System 2 relief). Giving permission to say no reduces pressure (psychological reactance), which actually increases honest yeses.
**Script:** *"I have 30 minutes. My goal is to see if we can help. If we're not a fit, I'll tell you and we'll part friends — and I'd ask the same from you. Fair?"*
**Stage:** Discovery
**Common mistakes:** Reciting it as a script; never invoking it later when the deal stalls.

### 13. Tonality as the First Message (Slow-Down/Drop-Down)
**Source:** Voss (late-night FM DJ voice) + Contreras / High-Ticket Canon (tonality control)
**Core idea:** Within the first 5 seconds, *how* you sound matters more than *what* you say. Use a calm, downward-inflected, slightly slower-than-normal voice — the "executive cadence."
**Why it works:** Vocal tone hits the limbic system before words are processed. A calm tone signals safety, mirroring the brain into a non-defensive state.
**How to apply:** Slow down 15–20%; drop pitch at the end of statements; eliminate uptalk.
**Script:** Same words, different tone: "Hey, [name]… thanks for picking up… real quick — this is a cold call." (downward, deliberate)
**Stage:** Every stage, especially cold open
**Common mistakes:** "Smiling-and-dialing" energy that reads as sales-y; uptalk on declarative statements.

### 14. The "Earned" Hook (Disarming Bluntness)
**Source:** 30MPC + Contreras / High-Ticket Canon
**Core idea:** When asked "how did you get my number?" or "what is this about?", answer with disarming honesty: "On the internet" / "It's a cold call." Don't hide.
**Why it works:** Honesty pattern-interrupts the script. It activates *liking* (Cialdini) — people trust those who admit small flaws (pratfall effect). It also collapses the BS-detector.
**Script:** *"Honestly? Got your number off ZoomInfo. This is a cold call — I'll be quick."*
**Stage:** Cold Call
**Common mistakes:** Lying, hedging, or pretending you have a "warm" connection that's clearly cold.

## CATEGORY 2 — RAPPORT & TRUST BUILDING (Concepts 15–28)

### 15. Mirroring (The Last 1–3 Words)
**Source:** Voss, *Never Split the Difference*
**Core idea:** Repeat the last 1–3 critical words your counterpart said, in a curious upward inflection — then go silent. They will reword and expand.
**Why it works:** Triggers reciprocal disclosure (System 1 fills silence with elaboration). Activates "feeling heard," which lowers defensiveness via the limbic system.
**How to apply:** Pick the most emotionally loaded or ambiguous word.
**Script:** Buyer: *"Honestly, our pipeline has been a disaster."* Rep: *"A disaster?"* (silence)
**Stage:** Discovery / Objection
**Common mistakes:** Mirroring every sentence (annoying); mirroring too long (becomes parroting); not pausing afterward.

### 16. Labeling Emotions
**Source:** Voss
**Core idea:** Name the emotion you sense ("It seems like…", "It sounds like…", "It looks like…") to validate it without agreeing with it. Defuses negative emotion and confirms positive emotion.
**Why it works:** fMRI research (Lieberman) shows verbalizing an emotion reduces amygdala activity. People shift from feeling the emotion to evaluating it.
**Script:** *"It sounds like you've been burned by a vendor before and you're not in a hurry to repeat that."*
**Stage:** Discovery / Objection
**Common mistakes:** Using "I feel like…" (puts focus on you); telling them how they feel ("You're frustrated") instead of inviting confirmation.

### 17. Tactical Empathy
**Source:** Voss
**Core idea:** Demonstrate that you understand the prospect's feelings *and* the reasoning behind them — without necessarily agreeing. It's not sympathy, it's diagnostic listening.
**Why it works:** People who feel deeply understood lower their guard, share Black Swan information, and move from positions to interests.
**Script:** *"If I'm in your shoes, two failed implementations in three years probably makes me extremely cautious about anyone promising the moon — that fair?"*
**Stage:** Discovery / Objection / Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Performing empathy ("I totally get it!") without demonstrating actual understanding; jumping to solutions before earning the empathy.

### 18. The "That's Right" vs "You're Right" Distinction
**Source:** Voss
**Core idea:** "You're right" means "Stop talking, I'm not changing my mind." "That's right" means "You actually understand me — proceed." Earn "that's right" via summary + labeling.
**Why it works:** "That's right" signals genuine cognitive alignment; only then will the prospect collaborate with your proposal.
**Script:** Rep summarizes pain in their words. Buyer: *"That's right."* Now the rep can suggest next steps.
**Stage:** Discovery / Demo / Closing
**Common mistakes:** Settling for "you're right"; rushing the summary; using your jargon instead of theirs.

### 19. The Accusation Audit
**Source:** Voss
**Core idea:** Pre-emptively name every negative thing your counterpart could be thinking about you, before they say it.
**Why it works:** Naming the negative voids its power (similar to labeling). Removes the prospect's need to be defensive — they don't have to "fight" what you've already conceded.
**Script:** *"You're probably thinking this is yet another rep with a magic-bullet pitch, that I haven't done my homework, and that this call is going to waste your time."*
**Stage:** Cold Call / Discovery / Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Doing it half-heartedly; adding only flattering "concerns"; following with a sales pitch that confirms the accusations.

### 20. Late-Night FM DJ Voice (Calm Authority)
**Source:** Voss
**Core idea:** A slow, downward-inflected, deliberately calm voice projects unshakable authority and emotional regulation.
**Why it works:** Vocal tone is processed by the limbic system before semantic content. Calm voices co-regulate the listener's nervous system (vocal contagion).
**How to apply:** Drop pitch on the last syllable; pause before key words; slow 15–20%.
**Stage:** Objection / Negotiation / Pricing
**Common mistakes:** Sounding sleepy or condescending; using it inappropriately when energy is needed (e.g., enthusiastic closes).

### 21. Strategic Mimicry / Mirroring Body Language
**Source:** Pink (*To Sell Is Human*); Pre-Suasion
**Core idea:** Subtly match the prospect's posture, energy, pace, and word choice. People trust those who feel like "one of us."
**Why it works:** Cialdini's *unity* principle — "we" before "I." Mimicry activates in-group safety circuits.
**How to apply:** Match volume and pace within ~70%; pick up on their key phrases ("you said your team is 'drowning' — what's drowning the team?").
**Stage:** Every stage
**Common mistakes:** Obvious mirroring (looks creepy); mimicking accents; overusing their slang.

### 22. The Pratfall Effect (Strategic Vulnerability)
**Source:** Behavioral psychology (Aronson) / Contreras / High-Ticket Canon
**Core idea:** Admitting a small flaw or limitation increases trust more than projecting flawlessness.
**Why it works:** Perfection triggers BS-detection. A small admission ("we're not the cheapest") makes everything else you say more credible by contrast.
**Script:** *"Just so we're upfront — we're probably not the cheapest option you'll evaluate. What we are is the only one that does X."*
**Stage:** Discovery / Demo / Pricing
**Common mistakes:** Disclosing real deal-killers; sounding self-deprecating instead of secure.

### 23. The 80/20 Talk-Listen Ratio
**Source:** Gong research / Chris Orlob (pclub.io) / SPIN
**Core idea:** Top performers in discovery talk ~43% of the time and listen ~57%. Average reps talk 65–75%. The best reps shut up and let prospects describe their pain.
**Why it works:** People love talking about themselves and their problems. Listening creates *the most prized commodity in selling: information.* Talking trains the rep's ear away from listening cues.
**How to apply:** After every question, count to 3 internally before responding. Use minimal encouragers ("uh-huh," "go on").
**Stage:** Discovery / Demo
**Common mistakes:** Confusing question-asking with listening; interrupting; "leading" with your bias instead of letting the prospect frame.

### 24. Minimal Encouragers
**Source:** Voss / active listening literature
**Core idea:** Short verbal nudges — "uh-huh," "I see," "go on," "tell me more" — keep the prospect talking without interrupting their train of thought.
**Why it works:** They signal attention without redirecting. Silence punctuated by tiny prompts feels safer than questions, which can feel like interrogation.
**Stage:** Discovery
**Common mistakes:** Saying "right, right, right" rapidly (signals impatience); inserting opinions disguised as encouragers.

### 25. The Unity Principle (Cialdini's 7th)
**Source:** Cialdini (*Pre-Suasion*)
**Core idea:** Influence is amplified when you and the prospect share a category identity ("we both ran sales teams," "we're both founders," "we both deal with this every day"). The shared "we" is more powerful than reciprocity or authority.
**Why it works:** In-group preference is a deep evolutionary instinct — we extend trust automatically to "our kind."
**How to apply:** Reference shared identity explicitly: profession, alma mater, experience, values.
**Script:** *"Look — as a fellow seller who's been on the wrong end of a botched implementation…"*
**Stage:** Rapport / Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Faking shared identity; making it tokenistic.

### 26. The Liking Principle (Genuine Compliments + Similarity)
**Source:** Cialdini, *Influence*
**Core idea:** People prefer to say yes to people they like. Liking is built through (a) similarity, (b) genuine compliments, (c) cooperation toward a shared goal.
**Why it works:** Halo effect — once liked, your messages bypass critical scrutiny.
**How to apply:** Before asking for anything, find one specific, genuine thing to admire (a recent talk, an article, a hire).
**Script:** *"Watched your talk at SaaStr — your point about second-order revenue effects is exactly why I called."*
**Stage:** Cold Outreach / Discovery
**Common mistakes:** Generic flattery ("Great LinkedIn!") which reads as cheap; over-complimenting (suspicious).

### 27. The Humbling Disclaimer
**Source:** 30MPC
**Core idea:** Before asking a high-friction question (e.g., "Who else needs to sign off?", "What's your budget?"), preface it with a disarming, slightly self-deprecating frame.
**Why it works:** Lowers psychological reactance — the prospect doesn't feel cornered. Activates *liking* through the pratfall effect.
**Script:** *"I feel a little silly asking this since we've had such a great conversation — but who else, besides you, would weigh in on a decision like this?"*
**Stage:** Discovery / Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Using it for non-friction questions (overuse devalues it).

### 28. Vulnerability-Driven Disclosure
**Source:** Pink (*To Sell Is Human*) — the new "Servant Selling"
**Core idea:** Share a small, relevant vulnerability (a story of a previous mistake, a concern about your own product) to invite reciprocal honesty.
**Why it works:** Reciprocity of disclosure — when you reveal something real, the prospect's social default is to match you. Builds *trust beyond rapport*.
**Script:** *"Honestly — when I joined, I was skeptical we could deliver in under 90 days. What changed my mind was [X]. Here's what to look out for."*
**Stage:** Discovery / Demo / Objection
**Common mistakes:** Performative vulnerability; oversharing; making it about you.

## CATEGORY 3 — DISCOVERY & QUESTIONING (Concepts 29–43)

### 29. SPIN — Situation Questions (Use Sparingly)
**Source:** Rackham, *SPIN Selling*
**Core idea:** Fact-finding questions about the prospect's current state ("How is your team structured?"). Necessary but heavily limit them — Rackham's data showed a *negative* correlation between situation-question count and call success.
**Why it works:** Prospects are bored answering questions you could have Googled. Pre-call research lets you confirm rather than discover situation facts.
**Script:** *"I see you have ~50 reps split across NA and EMEA — is that still right?"* (verifies, not interrogates)
**Stage:** Discovery
**Common mistakes:** Burning 10+ minutes on situation questions; asking what's already on the website.

### 30. SPIN — Problem Questions
**Source:** Rackham
**Core idea:** Surface implied needs by asking about difficulties, dissatisfactions, or pain in the current state.
**Why it works:** People are far more motivated to fix problems than chase gains (loss aversion). Problems uncover the "gap."
**Script:** *"Where are reps spending the most time that *isn't* selling?"* / *"What's the most frustrating part of your current pipeline review process?"*
**Stage:** Discovery
**Common mistakes:** Stopping at surface problems instead of digging.

### 31. SPIN — Implication Questions (The Win Driver)
**Source:** Rackham
**Core idea:** Connect the small problem to a much bigger downstream consequence. Top performers ask 4× more implication questions than average reps.
**Why it works:** Activates loss aversion and personalizes the cost. A 14-hour-a-week annoyance becomes a $400K/year revenue leak.
**Script:** *"If reps are losing 9 hours a week to admin… across 50 reps and a $400K quota each, what does that mean for revenue you're leaving on the table this year?"*
**Stage:** Discovery / Demo
**Common mistakes:** Skipping straight from problem to solution; asking implications as gotchas instead of genuine curiosity.

### 32. SPIN — Need-Payoff Questions
**Source:** Rackham
**Core idea:** Get the prospect to articulate the *value* of solving the problem in their own words (rather than you telling them).
**Why it works:** Self-persuasion via commitment/consistency. People believe their own conclusions far more than yours.
**Script:** *"If you got those 9 hours back per rep, what would you have them spend the time on?"* / *"What would solving this be worth quarterly?"*
**Stage:** Discovery → pre-Close
**Common mistakes:** Phrasing them as obvious leading questions ("Wouldn't it be great if…?"); asking before establishing real pain.

### 33. The Discovery Tree (Situation → Operational Pain → Executive Problem → Business Impact)
**Source:** 30MPC Discovery Course
**Core idea:** Map every line of questioning into 4 layers, then transition with "so what?" between layers. Gets you from low-stakes facts to executive-grade business impact.
**Why it works:** Forces multi-level value articulation; ensures you can sell to both end-user (operational) and executive (financial) buyers.
**Script:** Situation → "So how does that affect your team day-to-day?" → "So what does that mean for [VP]?" → "So what's the dollar impact of that?"
**Stage:** Discovery
**Common mistakes:** Stopping at operational pain; never climbing to executive impact (deals stall).

### 34. The "So What?" Transition
**Source:** 30MPC
**Core idea:** A simple verbal bridge that climbs the Discovery Tree. After every answer, internally ask "so what?" and verbalize a version of it.
**Why it works:** Forces the prospect to do the implication math themselves.
**Script:** Buyer: *"Reps spend 9 hours/week on data entry."* Rep: *"And what does that translate to in terms of pipeline?"*
**Stage:** Discovery
**Common mistakes:** Sounding incredulous ("So what?!"); skipping the transition altogether.

### 35. Bucket Questions (Pattern Anchors)
**Source:** 30MPC
**Core idea:** Frame discovery around the 2–3 most common pains your peers have ("Most COOs I talk to are dealing with X, Y, or Z — which bucket are you closest to?").
**Why it works:** Cognitive ease — prospects don't have to invent an answer; they pick from your menu. Also subtly establishes authority (you talk to many COOs).
**Script:** *"Most VPs of CS are wrestling with one of three things — onboarding speed, expansion attribution, or churn signals. Which one's loudest for you?"*
**Stage:** Discovery / Cold Call
**Common mistakes:** Listing buckets that aren't real pains; using it as a closed multiple-choice trap.

### 36. The Mom Test — Talk About Their Life, Not Your Idea
**Source:** Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test*
**Core idea:** Never ask "would you buy this?" Ask about their actual past behavior. Hypotheticals are worthless because people are wildly optimistic about future actions.
**Why it works:** Past behavior is the only honest predictor of future behavior. Hypothetical "yes" responses suffer from politeness bias.
**Script:** Bad: *"Would you use a tool that does X?"* Good: *"Walk me through the last time this came up. What did you do?"*
**Stage:** Discovery
**Common mistakes:** Pitching the idea then asking for opinions; accepting "I would totally buy that" as data.

### 37. Anchor the Fluff (Mom Test Rule #2)
**Source:** Fitzpatrick
**Core idea:** When prospects answer in generics ("we usually," "we always," "I would"), redirect to a specific past instance. Generics are bad data; specifics are gold.
**Why it works:** Specifics expose actual behavior; generics expose self-image.
**Script:** Buyer: *"We usually evaluate quarterly."* Rep: *"When was the last time you actually evaluated — like, what triggered it?"*
**Stage:** Discovery
**Common mistakes:** Accepting generics and moving on; sounding interrogative.

### 38. Deflect the Compliment (Mom Test Rule #1)
**Source:** Fitzpatrick
**Core idea:** Compliments are the fool's gold of customer learning. When a prospect says "this is great!" — don't celebrate; pivot to the *why* and re-anchor in concrete behavior.
**Why it works:** Compliments cost the prospect nothing and tell you nothing about purchase intent.
**Script:** *"Appreciate that — but what specifically would have to be true for you to actually use this next month?"*
**Stage:** Discovery / Demo
**Common mistakes:** Reading enthusiasm as commitment; building a forecast on compliments.

### 39. The Size-the-Problem Range Question
**Source:** 30MPC
**Core idea:** Prospects rarely volunteer real numbers. Offer extreme ranges to make them comfortable revealing the truth: "Are you closer to a 5% gap or a 70% gap?"
**Why it works:** Anchoring + permission. Wide ranges signal you won't judge, and they nudge the prospect into the middle (which is often the real number).
**Script:** *"Of all the deals slipping last quarter — are we talking 3 deals or 30?"*
**Stage:** Discovery
**Common mistakes:** Asking for exact figures upfront ("What's your churn rate?") which triggers defensiveness.

### 40. The Negative Impact Question (pclub.io / Chris Orlob)
**Source:** Chris Orlob, pclub.io
**Core idea:** After surfacing pain, ask explicitly about the *downstream negative impact* on revenue, headcount, morale, career, etc. Make the prospect verbalize the cost of inaction.
**Why it works:** Loss aversion is the most powerful motivator in B2B buying. Prospects must feel the pain of *not* changing more than the friction of changing.
**Script:** *"If this problem persists for another two quarters, what's the realistic worst-case for the team — and for you personally?"*
**Stage:** Discovery
**Common mistakes:** Skipping personal impact (it's where urgency lives); softening the question.

### 41. Gap Selling — Current State / Future State / Gap
**Source:** Keenan, *Gap Selling*
**Core idea:** Discovery has three jobs: define the prospect's *current state* (problems + impact + root cause + emotion), define their *future state* (where they want to be), and quantify the *gap* between. The bigger the gap, the bigger the deal.
**Why it works:** Buyers don't buy products; they buy *change*. Sale lives in the gap.
**How to apply:** Spend ~25% of total sales cycle on this triangulation.
**Stage:** Discovery
**Common mistakes:** Selling to "need" or "pain" instead of the *gap*; jumping to product before mapping current/future state.

### 42. The Pain Funnel (Sandler)
**Source:** Sandler Selling System
**Core idea:** A series of progressively deeper questions that take the prospect from technical pain ("the system goes down") to business impact ("we miss SLAs") to personal/emotional pain ("the CFO is breathing down my neck").
**Why it works:** Emotional pain (Level 3) is what drives buying urgency. Without it, deals stall.
**Funnel sequence:** Tell me more → Can you be more specific? → Give me an example → How long has this been a problem? → What have you tried? → How did that work? → How much has this cost you? → How do you feel about that?
**Stage:** Discovery
**Common mistakes:** Stopping at Level 1; asking the questions mechanically (it sounds like an interrogation).

### 43. The 1–10 Reality-Check Question
**Source:** 30MPC + general sales canon
**Core idea:** At the end of a demo or discovery, ask "On a scale of 1–10, how well does this solve your problem?" If the answer is anything less than 10, ask "What would make it a 10?"
**Why it works:** Surfaces hidden objections immediately. Numbers feel less confrontational than verbal critiques.
**Script:** *"On 1 to 10 — how close are we to what you actually need?"* → *"What's the gap between 7 and 10?"*
**Stage:** End of Discovery / Demo / pre-Close
**Common mistakes:** Accepting "8" as good enough; not following up the gap question.

## CATEGORY 4 — OBJECTION HANDLING (Concepts 44–57)

### 44. The LDA Framework — Ledge, Disrupt, Ask
**Source:** Blount, *Objections* / Fanatical Prospecting
**Core idea:** When hit with a Reflex, Brush-off, or Objection (RBO), use a 3-step turnaround: **Ledge** (a calm pre-set phrase to buy time), **Disrupt** (break their script), **Ask** (re-issue the request).
**Why it works:** RBOs are amygdala-driven script responses, not real objections. Disrupting the script forces System 2 engagement.
**Script:** Prospect: *"Not interested."* Rep: *"Most people aren't the first time I call (ledge) — that's exactly why I gave you a heads-up about [specific problem] (disrupt). Worth 15 minutes Thursday? (ask)"*
**Stage:** Cold Call / Objection
**Common mistakes:** Arguing with the objection instead of ledging; skipping the ask (no re-close).

### 45. The Acknowledge–Question–Reframe Loop
**Source:** Voss / 30MPC "Mr. Miyagi" objection method
**Core idea:** Don't fight an objection — flow with it. Acknowledge ("totally fair"), ask a clarifying question ("can you help me understand?"), then reframe.
**Why it works:** Acknowledging removes resistance; questioning lets you isolate the *real* objection (often hidden behind the stated one); reframing repositions without contradiction.
**Script:** Buyer: *"Too expensive."* Rep: *"Yeah — too expensive compared to what?"* (acknowledge + clarify) → *"If we set price aside for one second, is the value clear?"* (reframe)
**Stage:** Objection / Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Skipping the acknowledgment; reframing too fast (feels manipulative).

### 46. The Isolation Technique
**Source:** Sandler / general sales canon
**Core idea:** When you get an objection, isolate it: "Other than [objection], is there anything else stopping us from moving forward?" Surface every objection before answering any.
**Why it works:** Prevents whack-a-mole. Once the prospect commits to a finite list, answering them removes all barriers.
**Script:** *"Putting price aside, is there anything else that would stop you from saying yes today?"*
**Stage:** Closing
**Common mistakes:** Answering the first objection without isolating; sounding pushy ("So if I solve this, you'll buy?").

### 47. The Reversal (Sandler)
**Source:** Sandler
**Core idea:** Answer a question with a question to surface the real intent. "What's the price?" → "If the price made sense, would you be ready to move forward?"
**Why it works:** Most early questions are smokescreens. Reversals expose the real concern without giving away leverage.
**Script:** Buyer: *"Can you send a proposal?"* Rep: *"Happy to — what specifically would be in the proposal that would make this a yes?"*
**Stage:** Discovery / Closing
**Common mistakes:** Reversing every question (sounds evasive); reversing too aggressively (defensive).

### 48. Negative Reverse Selling (Sandler)
**Source:** Sandler
**Core idea:** Make a statement that implies the *opposite* of what you want, e.g., "It sounds like maybe this isn't the right time for you" — to provoke the prospect into arguing for the deal.
**Why it works:** Psychological reactance. When you "take it away," the prospect defends their interest.
**Script:** *"Honestly, given everything you've said, I'm not sure we're a fit — should we just stop here?"*
**Stage:** Stalled deals / Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Sounding sarcastic; using it on prospects who are already disengaged (they'll just agree).

### 49. The Loss-Aversion Reframe
**Source:** Kahneman / Tversky / Cialdini
**Core idea:** Reframe the value proposition as the cost of *not* changing rather than the gain of changing. Losses loom 2x larger than equivalent gains.
**Script:** Don't say: *"You'll save $200K/year."* Say: *"Right now, you're losing $200K/year to this — every quarter you wait, that's another $50K out the door."*
**Stage:** Discovery / Demo / Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Layering loss frames so heavily it feels like fear-mongering.

### 50. The Price Objection Sequence (Defuse-Reframe-Anchor)
**Source:** Voss + 30MPC + Sales canon
**Core idea:** "Too expensive" is rarely about price — it's about value. Sequence: (1) defuse with a label ("Sounds like the investment feels off"), (2) reframe to value ("What were you comparing it to?"), (3) re-anchor in ROI ("The cost of *not* fixing this is X").
**Stage:** Negotiation / Closing
**Common mistakes:** Discounting immediately; defending price with feature lists.

### 51. The "Compared to What?" Pivot
**Source:** Pricing psychology / Ariely (relativity)
**Core idea:** When prospects say something is too expensive, don't defend the price — ask what they're comparing to. Almost always it's a flawed anchor (cheaper competitor, doing nothing, free option).
**Why it works:** Forces the comparison out into the open where you can correct it.
**Script:** *"Too expensive compared to what — to a competitor, to your budget, or to staying where you are?"*
**Stage:** Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Assuming you know the comparison.

### 52. The "Just Send Me an Email" Disarm
**Source:** 30MPC
**Core idea:** When a cold prospect brushes you off with "send me an email," respond: "Happy to — quick one so I don't have to call again: is it that you have no budget, or that this just isn't a priority?"
**Why it works:** Forces a real answer that disqualifies fast or surfaces the actual blocker. Status quo bias is exposed.
**Stage:** Cold Call
**Common mistakes:** Sending the email and never following up; sounding combative.

### 53. The "I Need to Think About It" Surfacing
**Source:** Sandler / 30MPC
**Core idea:** "I need to think about it" is the most expensive phrase in sales — it usually means there's an unspoken objection. Surface it: "Totally — what's the biggest thing you're going to be thinking about?"
**Why it works:** Forces specificity. Prospects either name a real objection (which you can handle) or admit there isn't one.
**Stage:** Closing
**Common mistakes:** Accepting it and disappearing; pushing to close without surfacing.

### 54. The Feel-Felt-Found Reframe
**Source:** Classic sales / cited in Cialdini's *liking*
**Core idea:** Acknowledge the prospect's feeling, share that others felt the same, then describe what they found.
**Why it works:** Validation + social proof + outcome.
**Script:** *"I hear you — most CFOs I talk to feel the same about price. What they've found, after the first 90 days, is that the ROI is roughly 4x the licensing cost."*
**Stage:** Objection
**Common mistakes:** Sounding like a script.

### 55. The Status Quo Reframe
**Source:** Behavioral econ / Corporate Visions research
**Core idea:** ~40–60% of B2B deals end in "no decision" (status quo wins). Specifically frame the *cost of inaction*: "Doing nothing isn't free. It costs $X every quarter."
**Why it works:** Status quo bias is invisible to most buyers — they don't see staying as a "decision." Naming it forces them to weigh both options.
**Script:** *"What does it cost you to do nothing for another 6 months?"*
**Stage:** Closing
**Common mistakes:** Selling against competitors instead of selling against inaction.

### 56. The Conditional Close (Trade Question)
**Source:** Voss / negotiation canon
**Core idea:** Whenever an objection asks for a concession, attach a condition: "If I could solve [X], would you be in a position to move forward this week?"
**Why it works:** Earns commitment in exchange for resolution. Prevents one-way concessions.
**Script:** *"If I can get implementation done in 30 days instead of 60, are we going to be ready to sign by Friday?"*
**Stage:** Closing / Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Conceding before getting commitment; vague "if/then" without specifics.

### 57. The "How Am I Supposed to Do That?" Calibrated Pushback
**Source:** Voss
**Core idea:** When asked for an unreasonable concession, ask "How am I supposed to do that?" — calmly, with no edge.
**Why it works:** Forces the counterpart to solve your problem. Often they reveal flexibility you didn't know existed.
**Script:** Buyer: *"We need 50% off."* Rep: *"How am I supposed to do that and keep the project profitable?"*
**Stage:** Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Sounding combative or whiny; not pausing afterward.

## CATEGORY 5 — CLOSING & COMMITMENT (Concepts 58–71)

### 58. The Trial Close
**Source:** Sales canon / Pink
**Core idea:** Throughout the conversation, gently test buying temperature without forcing a decision: "How does this feel so far?" / "Does this match what you were hoping to see?"
**Why it works:** Reveals readiness; gathers small commitments (consistency principle); avoids the "big ask" surprise at the end.
**Stage:** Demo / pre-Close
**Common mistakes:** Mistaking polite "yeses" for buying intent; over-trial-closing.

### 59. The Assumptive Close
**Source:** Sales canon
**Core idea:** Move forward as if the decision is made: "I'll send the agreement over this afternoon — what's the best email?"
**Why it works:** Cognitive ease (Kahneman) — assumes a path of least resistance. Skips the binary "yes/no" cliff.
**Script:** *"Sounds like we're aligned — should I send paperwork your way today, or is tomorrow better?"*
**Stage:** Closing
**Common mistakes:** Using it before earning the right (premature); ignoring real hesitation.

### 60. The Alternative-Choice Close
**Source:** Sales canon / Cialdini commitment
**Core idea:** Offer two options, both of which imply purchase: "Annual or quarterly? Standard or pro?"
**Why it works:** Choice architecture — buyers find decisions between two options easier than yes/no.
**Stage:** Closing
**Common mistakes:** Three or more options (paralysis); both options feeling forced.

### 61. The Summary Close
**Source:** Sales canon
**Core idea:** Recap every problem the prospect has named, every benefit you've agreed solves it, and ask: "Anything missing before we move forward?"
**Why it works:** Activates commitment/consistency — the prospect has agreed to each piece individually; the sum follows.
**Stage:** Closing
**Common mistakes:** Reading from a list; summarizing *your* points instead of theirs.

### 62. The "Let's Get the Calendar Out" Next-Step Close
**Source:** 30MPC
**Core idea:** Never end a meeting without scheduling the next concrete step on calendars, in real time. "I'll follow up" = the deal is dead.
**Why it works:** Calendar bookings are micro-commitments that resist decay. Every unscheduled deal becomes a forecast risk.
**Script:** *"Before we wrap — let's pull up calendars now and get our follow-up booked. What's better, Tuesday or Thursday?"*
**Stage:** Every meeting
**Common mistakes:** Saying "I'll send some times"; accepting "let's circle back."

### 63. The Give-to-Get Mindset
**Source:** 30MPC
**Core idea:** Every time you give something (demo, custom POC, proposal), explicitly ask for something in return (intro to power, signed MSA, timeline commitment).
**Why it works:** Reciprocity (Cialdini) + activity-as-achievement guard. Prevents one-way concessions and signals you value your time.
**Script:** *"Happy to put together a custom ROI model — in exchange, can you set up a 20-minute call with your CFO so we make sure we're modeling the right metrics?"*
**Stage:** Demo / Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Giving away resources (POCs, custom decks) without trading.

### 64. Problem Agreement / Solution Agreement / Power Agreement
**Source:** 30MPC three-stage cycle
**Core idea:** Don't move a deal forward until: (1) the prospect explicitly agrees the problem is a priority, (2) they agree your solution is the *best* way to solve it, (3) the actual decision-maker (Power) has agreed to both. Skip any and the deal dies.
**Why it works:** Each is a verbal commitment that locks in consistency. Moves deals forward on outcomes, not activity.
**Stage:** Demo through Closing
**Common mistakes:** Mistaking interest for problem agreement; closing without power agreement.

### 65. The MEDDPICC Champion Test
**Source:** MEDDPICC (Dunkel/Napoli, PTC)
**Core idea:** A real Champion has (1) power and influence, (2) personal benefit from the deal, (3) access to the Economic Buyer, and (4) is willing to *sell internally* on your behalf. Test by asking them to take an action — get a meeting with the EB, share an internal email, run an internal calculation.
**Why it works:** Unsolicited cooperation is the only valid signal of a real champion. Without one, deals stall.
**Script:** *"Would you be open to setting up a 20-minute joint call with your CFO so we can pressure-test this together?"*
**Stage:** Mid-cycle / Closing
**Common mistakes:** Believing a low-power friendly contact is a Champion ("coach," not Champion).

### 66. Multi-Threading (Above-the-Line Decision Maker)
**Source:** 30MPC, MEDDPICC, Challenger
**Core idea:** "A deal without an above-the-line decision-maker is a dead deal." Always be widening — prospect peers, executive sponsors, technical buyers, finance.
**Why it works:** B2B buying groups average 6–10 stakeholders (Gartner). Single-threaded deals churn when your contact leaves or pivots.
**How to apply:** CEO-to-CEO mirror outreach; ask the Champion to introduce you to the EB on a joint call.
**Stage:** Throughout cycle
**Common mistakes:** Single-threading on a friendly mid-level Champion; never asking for power introductions.

### 67. The Mutual Action Plan (MAP) / Mutual Close Plan
**Source:** Modern enterprise sales (MEDDPICC, 30MPC)
**Core idea:** Co-build a written, dated, milestone plan from current state → signature, sent to all stakeholders. Every commit has an owner and a date.
**Why it works:** Commitment/consistency at the document level; surfaces blockers (legal, security) early; converts vague "Q4 close" into concrete next steps.
**Stage:** Mid- to Late-cycle
**Common mistakes:** Building a one-sided seller-only "close plan"; never updating it.

### 68. Look for Trouble (Pre-mortem Close)
**Source:** 30MPC + decision science
**Core idea:** Before closing, proactively ask: "Why *wouldn't* you want to do this?" / "What's the most likely reason this falls apart in procurement?"
**Why it works:** Surfaces hidden objections while you can still address them. Pre-mortem reasoning improves deal hygiene.
**Script:** *"Putting myself in your shoes — what would make me say 'we're not ready' two weeks from now?"*
**Stage:** Pre-Closing
**Common mistakes:** Avoiding the question for fear of triggering objections (they'll appear anyway, just later).

### 69. Urgency Through Compelling Events (Not Manufactured Scarcity)
**Source:** MEDDPICC + general
**Core idea:** Real urgency comes from a *compelling event* in the prospect's world (board meeting, contract renewal, new fiscal year, regulatory deadline) — not from your "end-of-quarter pricing."
**Why it works:** Prospects see through manufactured discount-driven urgency. Compelling events create real cost-of-delay math.
**Script:** *"You mentioned the board reviews these numbers in March — backing into that, when would we need to be live?"*
**Stage:** Closing
**Common mistakes:** Creating fake EOQ urgency; missing the buyer's actual deadline.

### 70. Micro-Commitments (Foot-in-the-Door)
**Source:** Cialdini commitment/consistency
**Core idea:** Small early commitments (a 15-minute call, a security review, a pilot) make later large commitments feel consistent with prior behavior.
**Why it works:** Self-perception theory — once people act, they update their identity to match.
**Stage:** Throughout cycle
**Common mistakes:** Skipping small commitments and asking for the big one; banking too many small yeses without driving the big ask.

### 71. The Reinforcement Close (Post-Sale Lock-In)
**Source:** Contreras / High-Ticket Canon (CLOSER framework "R" — Reinforce); Cialdini commitment
**Core idea:** Immediately after the close, reinforce the buyer's decision. Recap *their* reasons for buying, congratulate them, give them a clear first action.
**Why it works:** Prevents buyer's remorse and post-decision dissonance. Locks in commitment so cancellation feels inconsistent with their stated reasons.
**Script:** *"You just made the decision your team needs. Here's what happens next — and remember, the reason you said yes is [X]. Stay connected to that."*
**Stage:** Post-Close
**Common mistakes:** Ghosting after signature; never reinforcing the *why*.

## CATEGORY 6 — NEGOTIATION (Concepts 72–85)

### 72. The First Anchor (Make the First Offer When You Have Information)
**Source:** Negotiation research (Galinsky); Voss
**Core idea:** When you have good market data, anchor first with a number above your target. The opening number disproportionately shapes the final outcome.
**Why it works:** Anchoring bias (Kahneman/Tversky) — counterparts adjust insufficiently from the anchor. Even known-arbitrary anchors influence final price.
**Stage:** Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Letting the buyer anchor first when you have the data; anchoring without justification.

### 73. Anchor Defusing (Reject Without Counter-Offering)
**Source:** Galinsky / 30MPC
**Core idea:** When the buyer drops an aggressive low anchor, *do not counter immediately*. First defuse the anchor: "We're miles apart on that number." Only then counter.
**Why it works:** Counter-offering legitimizes the buyer's anchor as the lower bound of the bargaining zone. Defusing first resets the zone.
**Script:** *"I want to be respectful — but that number isn't realistic given the value on the table. Let's step back."*
**Stage:** Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Splitting the difference too quickly; getting emotional.

### 74. Win Vendor of Choice First, Negotiate Price Last
**Source:** 30MPC + classic negotiation
**Core idea:** Never negotiate price until the buyer has confirmed *you* are the vendor they want. Otherwise you become free leverage for a competitor's discount.
**Why it works:** Price discussions before commitment turn you into a comparison shopper's pawn.
**Script:** *"Before we go to terms — if pricing works, are we your vendor of choice? I want to make sure I'm not just a stalking horse."*
**Stage:** Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Discounting reactively when the buyer hints at competition.

### 75. Concession Trading (Never Give Without Getting)
**Source:** Negotiation canon
**Core idea:** Every concession (price, terms, scope) must be paired with an ask in return (multi-year, signed-by date, references, expanded seats).
**Why it works:** Reciprocity + perceived value. Free concessions devalue the deal in the buyer's eyes; traded concessions reinforce value.
**Script:** *"I can do 12% off — if we move from a 1-year to a 2-year deal."*
**Stage:** Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Discounting "to make it easy"; one-way concessions; generous "best and final" without trade.

### 76. Shrinking Concessions (Diminishing Returns Pattern)
**Source:** Voss / Ackerman bargaining model
**Core idea:** Each successive concession should be smaller than the last (e.g., 10%, then 4%, then 1.5%). The pattern signals you're approaching your floor.
**Why it works:** Pattern recognition — the buyer's System 1 perceives diminishing room and stops pushing.
**Stage:** Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Equal-step concessions (signals there's always more); large final concession (signals you held back).

### 77. Justified Pricing (The "Because" Effect)
**Source:** Langer's classic copier study; PON Harvard
**Core idea:** Always justify your price or your refusal to discount with a reason — even a thin one. People accept refusals far more readily when "because" is attached.
**Why it works:** "Because" triggers compliance heuristic — humans accept reasoned refusals as legitimate.
**Script:** *"I can't go lower because our delivery model includes 90 days of dedicated onboarding — that's the line."*
**Stage:** Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Saying "no" without context; over-justifying weak positions.

### 78. The Walk-Away Anchor (BATNA)
**Source:** Negotiation canon / Fisher & Ury
**Core idea:** Know your *Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement* before walking in. Without a BATNA, every concession feels mandatory.
**Why it works:** Confidence and walk-away credibility come from knowing you don't *need* this deal.
**How to apply:** Pre-call: define minimum acceptable terms. Verbalize internally: "If they won't do X, I'm walking."
**Stage:** Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Reps internalizing the sale as essential; never modeling BATNA.

### 79. Fairness as Frame ("I Want You to Feel Treated Fairly")
**Source:** Voss
**Core idea:** Open negotiation by inviting fairness explicitly: "I want you to feel like you're being treated fairly at all times — please call me out if I'm not, and I'll do the same."
**Why it works:** Pre-empts the F-word weapon. People who say "fair" first own the moral high ground.
**Stage:** Negotiation opening
**Common mistakes:** Using it once and never invoking it again.

### 80. Loss-Frame Pricing (Gain → Loss Conversion)
**Source:** Tversky/Kahneman
**Core idea:** Reframe the same number as a loss avoided. "Save $100K" is weaker than "Stop losing $100K."
**Why it works:** Losses loom 2x larger than gains.
**Script:** Don't: *"This delivers $500K of value."* Do: *"Each quarter you delay, $125K walks out the door."*
**Stage:** Demo / Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Over-using fear (becomes counterproductive); ignoring loss-framing entirely.

### 81. The Decoy Effect in Pricing (3-Tier Architecture)
**Source:** Ariely (*Predictably Irrational*); Economist subscription study
**Core idea:** Present three options where the middle option is *asymmetrically dominant* — clearly better than the cheapest, almost as good as the most expensive but at lower cost. Most buyers pick the middle.
**Why it works:** Buyers don't evaluate in absolute terms — they compare. The decoy nudges them toward the option you want sold.
**How to apply:** Build Good/Better/Best pricing where Better is the bullseye.
**Stage:** Pricing / Proposal
**Common mistakes:** Three options that aren't comparable; anchoring on "Good" as the default.

### 82. Contrast Principle (Price Anchor Sequencing)
**Source:** Cialdini / Ariely
**Core idea:** Always quote your highest tier first; everything that follows feels cheaper by comparison.
**Why it works:** Sequence-of-comparison effect — the second number is judged against the first.
**Script:** *"Enterprise is $120K. Most customers in your segment are on Pro at $48K, which gets you 90% of the value."*
**Stage:** Pricing / Proposal
**Common mistakes:** Leading with cheapest tier (anchors low); listing tiers low-to-high.

### 83. The "Give Price and Shut Up" Principle
**Source:** 30MPC / negotiation canon
**Core idea:** After stating price, *stop talking*. The first person to speak loses leverage.
**Why it works:** Silence is uncomfortable; the buyer's System 1 fills it with self-justification or commitment.
**Stage:** Pricing
**Common mistakes:** Filling silence with discounts ("…but I can come down"); over-explaining.

### 84. The Three Whys of Negotiation (Why buy anything? Why buy us? Why buy now?)
**Source:** 30MPC
**Core idea:** Before any price negotiation, you must have answers to all three Whys. Missing any one and you're either negotiating with a tire-kicker or in a discount war.
**Why it works:** Each Why corresponds to a different blocker (status quo, competition, urgency).
**Stage:** Pre-Negotiation diagnostic
**Common mistakes:** Negotiating without all three answered.

### 85. The Ackerman Bargaining Model (Voss)
**Source:** Voss
**Core idea:** A structured counter-offer escalation: set target, open at 65% of target, escalate to 85%, 95%, 100%. Pair the final offer with a non-monetary concession plus a precise (non-rounded) number.
**Why it works:** Precise numbers (e.g., $87,432) read as carefully calculated and immovable. Non-monetary throw-ins (training, success criteria) preserve margin.
**Stage:** Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Round numbers (signal flexibility); skipping the non-monetary throw-in.

## CATEGORY 7 — PSYCHOLOGY & HUMAN NATURE (Concepts 86–100)

### 86. System 1 vs System 2 (Sell to Both)
**Source:** Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*
**Core idea:** ~95% of decisions are driven by System 1 (fast, emotional, automatic). System 2 (slow, deliberate) rationalizes after the fact. Top sellers trigger System 1 buying signals (emotion, social proof, vivid imagery) and equip System 2 with the rationale (ROI, security, references).
**How to apply:** Lead with story and pain (System 1); back up with numbers and case studies (System 2).
**Stage:** All
**Common mistakes:** Selling only to System 2 (technical decks, ROI spreadsheets) — buyers feel nothing; or only to System 1 (hype) — buyers can't justify internally.

### 87. Loss Aversion (2:1 Asymmetry)
**Source:** Kahneman/Tversky
**Core idea:** Losses feel approximately twice as powerful as equivalent gains. Reframe value propositions as losses avoided whenever possible.
**Why it works:** Evolutionary asymmetry — avoiding harm was more critical than acquiring benefit.
**Application:** Convert "save 30%" → "stop losing 30%." Convert "10% lift" → "you're leaving 10% on the table every quarter."
**Stage:** Discovery / Demo / Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Overusing fear; ignoring it entirely.

### 88. Anchoring Bias
**Source:** Kahneman/Tversky
**Core idea:** The first number in any negotiation disproportionately influences the final outcome — even when known to be arbitrary.
**Application:** Set price anchors high; defuse buyer anchors before counter-offering.
**Stage:** Pricing / Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Letting prospects anchor with "what we paid the last vendor" or "what's our budget."

### 89. The Availability Heuristic
**Source:** Kahneman/Tversky
**Core idea:** People judge frequency/probability by how easily examples come to mind. Make your category and your brand the most "available" thought.
**Application:** Vivid case studies, recent peer outcomes, named customers in the prospect's segment. "Three companies in your space had this same issue last quarter."
**Stage:** Cold Call / Discovery / Demo
**Common mistakes:** Generic case studies; outdated examples.

### 90. Reciprocity (Cialdini Principle 1)
**Source:** Cialdini, *Influence*
**Core idea:** People feel a deep obligation to return favors. The most powerful reciprocity is **personalized, unexpected, and meaningful** (Dennis Regan's restaurant tip study showed 23% lift).
**Application:** Pre-call insights, custom benchmarks, intro to a peer, free audit. Give first.
**Stage:** Outreach / Discovery
**Common mistakes:** "Free trial" as reciprocity (it's table stakes); transactional gifts.

### 91. Commitment & Consistency (Cialdini Principle 2)
**Source:** Cialdini
**Core idea:** Once people commit (verbally, in writing, publicly), they align future behavior to be consistent with the commitment.
**Application:** Get small commitments throughout the cycle — agree on the problem, then on the impact, then on the solution criteria. By close, they're already 80% committed.
**Stage:** All
**Common mistakes:** Skipping micro-commitments and asking only for the macro yes.

### 92. Social Proof (Cialdini Principle 3)
**Source:** Cialdini
**Core idea:** People look to similar others for behavioral cues. The closer the proof is to the prospect's identity (same role, same industry, same company size), the stronger.
**Application:** Use peer references early ("here's what 3 other VPs of Sales at $100M-$500M SaaS companies did"); avoid generic logos.
**Stage:** Cold / Discovery / Closing
**Common mistakes:** Showing logos that aren't relevant peers; bragging about famous customers who don't resemble the buyer.

### 93. Authority (Cialdini Principle 4)
**Source:** Cialdini
**Core idea:** People defer to perceived experts. Authority is built through credentials, demonstrated knowledge, and confident framing. Even small symbols (titles, white papers, third-party data) compound.
**Application:** Lead with insight ("Gartner data shows…"), share a contrarian point of view, demonstrate market understanding before pitching.
**Stage:** Cold / Discovery / Demo
**Common mistakes:** Citing self-serving research; performing authority without earning it.

### 94. Scarcity (Cialdini Principle 5)
**Source:** Cialdini
**Core idea:** Things that are rare or time-limited become more desirable. Real scarcity (capacity, deadline) outperforms manufactured scarcity (fake EOQ pricing).
**Application:** Limited onboarding slots, version-locked pricing, expiring incentives tied to a real timeline.
**Stage:** Closing
**Common mistakes:** Crying wolf with monthly "last chance" deadlines.

### 95. Liking (Cialdini Principle 6)
**Source:** Cialdini
**Core idea:** People say yes to people they like. Liking is built on similarity, genuine compliments, and cooperation.
**Application:** Find authentic shared ground; compliment something specific; frame yourself as helping them, not selling.
**Stage:** All
**Common mistakes:** Faking similarity; over-flattering.

### 96. Unity (Cialdini Principle 7)
**Source:** Cialdini, *Pre-Suasion*
**Core idea:** Beyond liking — shared identity ("we") is the strongest form of influence. "We" beats "I" every time.
**Application:** Reference shared category identity — "as a fellow operator," "as someone who's been in your seat."
**Stage:** All
**Common mistakes:** Overclaiming kinship that isn't real.

### 97. The Sunk Cost Fallacy (Use it as a Reframe)
**Source:** Behavioral economics
**Core idea:** Buyers continue investing in failing tools/vendors to justify past spend. Reframe: "The money you've already spent is gone either way — the only question is whether you continue to bleed."
**Application:** Help buyers separate past investment from future ROI. *"What you've spent on [legacy vendor] is sunk — what matters is the next 12 months."*
**Stage:** Discovery / Demo / Negotiation
**Common mistakes:** Attacking their past decision (triggers ego defense); not naming the trap.

### 98. The Decoy / Asymmetric Dominance Effect
**Source:** Ariely; Huber, Payne, Puto (1981)
**Core idea:** Adding a third, slightly inferior option ("decoy") to two competing options nudges buyers toward the option that asymmetrically dominates the decoy.
**Application:** In Good/Better/Best pricing, design "Good" so that "Better" looks asymmetrically superior at modest extra cost.
**Stage:** Pricing
**Common mistakes:** Decoy that's too obviously bad (looks rigged).

### 99. The Cost of Free (Zero Price Effect)
**Source:** Ariely
**Core idea:** Free isn't just "cheap" — it triggers an irrational positive emotional response that distorts comparison. A free trial / free audit dramatically out-performs a 99%-discounted one.
**Application:** Open with a high-value free asset (custom audit, benchmark, peer intro) before the paid ask.
**Stage:** Outreach / Pre-pitch
**Common mistakes:** Wasting "free" on low-value freebies (whitepapers); never converting it into reciprocity.

### 100. Servant Selling — The Final Mindset (Pink + Contreras / High-Ticket Canon)
**Source:** Pink, *To Sell Is Human* (Servant Selling); Contreras / Impact Team's "decouple closing rate from guesswork — sell to actually help."
**Core idea:** The highest-performing modern sellers are not closers — they are problem-finders and decision-coaches. Genuinely take the position: "If this isn't right for you, I'll tell you, even if it costs me the deal." Selling becomes service.
**Why it works:** In a world of buyer information parity, the only durable advantage is being *the most useful person in the room*. Servant selling activates reciprocity, liking, authority, *and* unity simultaneously — and it produces happier post-sale customers (lower churn, higher referrals).
**How to apply:** Disqualify generously. Recommend competitors when honest. Lead with diagnosis. Tie every recommendation to *their* outcome, not your quota.
**Script:** *"Look — based on what you've told me, I don't think we're the best fit right now. Here's what I'd actually do in your shoes…"*
**Stage:** All
**Common mistakes:** Faking servanthood while still pushing the deal; using "service" language without the underlying mindset (buyers smell it instantly).
